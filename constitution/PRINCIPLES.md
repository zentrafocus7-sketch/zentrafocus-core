# ZentraFocus Core — Principles

These are the constitutional laws of ZentraFocus Core. Every future document, product, automation, AI agent, and business decision is obligated to obey them. A principle that cannot resolve a hard future case has no place here — each one below is written to survive contact with a difficult, specific decision, not just an easy one.

---

## 1. Primacy of Revenue

**Statement:** When priorities conflict, they resolve in this fixed order: revenue, customer value, speed of execution, simplicity, long-term scalability, documentation. This order is never reversed.

**Why it exists:** Without a fixed order, every prioritization debate is re-litigated from scratch. A young, growing company that argues about priorities every time loses more to indecision than to any single wrong call.

**Practical implication:** A proposal that improves documentation or scalability at the cost of a near-term revenue or customer opportunity loses by default, regardless of who proposes it or how well-argued it is.

---

## 2. Single Point of Truth

**Statement:** Every governed fact, decision, or rule exists in exactly one authoritative location. Every other reference to it points back, and never holds an independent copy.

**Why it exists:** A copy is a promise to keep two things in sync forever. That promise is broken silently, and by the time anyone notices, the copy has already misled someone.

**Practical implication:** Pricing, policy, and decisions may be referenced anywhere, but restated nowhere. A second copy of a governed fact is treated as a defect, not a convenience.

---

## 3. Irreversibility of Record

**Statement:** A decision can be changed. The record that it was made — what, when, why, by whom — is never deleted or rewritten, only superseded by a new record.

**Why it exists:** Institutional memory is the one thing a growing company cannot rebuild once lost. The value of a decision log is in trusting that it has never been quietly edited after the fact.

**Practical implication:** Correcting or reversing a past decision always produces a new, dated record that references the old one. The old one stays exactly as it was.

---

## 4. Authority Must Be Explicit

**Statement:** Every change to governed material must be traceable to a named authority level that was actually required for it — never inferred from the fact that someone happened to have access.

**Why it exists:** Access and authority drift apart as headcount grows. A system that treats "could" as "was allowed to" accumulates silent authority creep until no one can say who is actually in charge of what.

**Practical implication:** Having the technical ability to change something is never sufficient justification for having changed it. Every governed change names the authority that approved it.

---

## 5. Least Privilege by Default

**Statement:** Every human, contractor, and AI agent holds the minimum access their current function requires — never broad access granted for convenience, seniority, or trust.

**Why it exists:** At solo-founder scale, over-provisioned access is harmless because one person is watching everything. Past that scale, it is the single largest source of catastrophic error.

**Practical implication:** Access is granted per function and revoked when the function ends. Seniority, tenure, and trust are never, by themselves, grounds for broader access.

---

## 6. Humans and Agents Are Governed Identically

**Statement:** No rule applies to an AI agent that would not apply to a human in the same role, and no rule applies to a human that would not apply to an agent. The only legitimate difference between them is the scope of what they are authorized to act on.

**Why it exists:** Automation is otherwise treated as exempt from scrutiny simply because it's fast and doesn't ask for anything, which makes it the easiest path for ungoverned change to enter the system.

**Practical implication:** An automation that changes governed material follows the exact same review standard as a person would for the same change — never a lighter one because it moved faster.

---

## 7. Nothing Is True Until It Is Recorded

**Statement:** A decision or policy that exists only in conversation, memory, or a chat thread is not yet a decision. It becomes binding only once it is committed to the governed record.

**Why it exists:** "We talked about it" does not survive a hire, a handoff, or a year. A company that runs on remembered agreements has no continuity once more than one person is involved.

**Practical implication:** Verbal or informal agreements are never acted on as policy until someone formalizes them into a record. Until then, they carry no authority.

---

## 8. Reversion to Simplicity

**Statement:** When two designs satisfy the same requirement equally well, the simpler one is chosen — even when the more complex one is more elegant, more impressive, or more "correct" in the abstract.

**Why it exists:** Complexity is cheap to add and expensive to remove. Simplicity is the only property of a system that reliably gets cheaper to maintain over time; everything else gets more expensive.

**Practical implication:** Choosing the complex option requires naming a specific, current requirement the simple one fails to meet. "It's more robust" or "we might need it" is not sufficient.

---

## 9. Structure for Fission, Not Fusion

**Statement:** Systems are built as a single unified whole at current scale, but along seams that would let any one piece be separated out independently later. They are never pre-split in anticipation of growth that hasn't happened yet.

**Why it exists:** Splitting too early adds coordination cost the company hasn't earned. Building with no internal seams at all means a forced rewrite the moment growth demands separation — usually at the worst possible time to do one.

**Practical implication:** A new product, team, or automation is built inside the existing system, but never so entangled with it that it couldn't later be pulled out on its own.

---

## 10. Live Data Is Never Stored as Static Truth

**Statement:** Revenue, metrics, and other live business data are never copied into the governed record as if current. They are either referenced live from their system of record, or stored explicitly labeled as a dated, historical snapshot.

**Why it exists:** A number that could go stale, and isn't labeled as having a date, will eventually be trusted by someone after it stops being true. That's how bad decisions get made confidently.

**Practical implication:** Any figure appearing in Core is either live-linked or carries a visible timestamp identifying it as a snapshot, never presented as current fact.

---

## 11. Every Addition Must Reduce Future Ambiguity

**Statement:** New material is admitted to the governed record only if it makes a specific future decision easier or clearer. Material that doesn't serve a decision doesn't belong, regardless of its quality.

**Why it exists:** Documentation grows for its own sake by default. A record that grows without this discipline eventually gets so large that nobody reads it, which is functionally the same as having no record at all.

**Practical implication:** Anyone proposing to add to Core must be able to name the future decision it will make easier. If they can't, it doesn't get added.

---

## 12. No Rule Changes Without a Stated Reason

**Statement:** Standing principles and past decisions are presumed correct and are not casually revisited. They change only when someone states a specific reason or presents new evidence — never on preference alone.

**Why it exists:** A company that re-litigates every past decision on a whim never builds momentum. A company that can never revisit a decision regardless of new evidence calcifies and dies slowly instead.

**Practical implication:** "I'd rather it worked differently" does not authorize a change. "Here is the specific evidence that has changed" does.

---

## 13. One Fact, One Owner

**Statement:** Every governed fact has exactly one accountable owner at any given time — a defined role, not a group — responsible for its accuracy.

**Why it exists:** A fact owned by "the team" is owned by no one. Shared ownership is where facts silently rot first, because no single person feels responsible for catching it.

**Practical implication:** As the company grows past the founder being the default owner of everything, every governed fact is explicitly assigned to a named role before it's treated as reliable.

---

## 14. Rigor Scales With Reversibility

**Statement:** The scrutiny a decision requires is determined by how hard it would be to undo — not by who is making it, how confident they feel, or how much time is available.

**Why it exists:** This is the only objective way to calibrate how much process a decision deserves. Otherwise, cheap and reversible decisions get bogged down in unnecessary review, while expensive and irreversible ones slip through because someone senior made them quickly.

**Practical implication:** A reversible test or a minor content choice needs almost no review. A pricing philosophy change, a governance edit, or anything hard to undo needs the highest review this system provides, no matter who requests it or how obvious it seems.

---

## 15. The Record Outlives Its Author

**Statement:** Everything written into Core must be understandable by someone with zero prior context — a new hire, a new agent, or the founder years later having forgotten the details — not only by the person who wrote it.

**Why it exists:** Over a ten-year horizon, the context in any one person's head evaporates. A record that only makes sense "if you were there" has already failed at the one thing Core exists to do.

**Practical implication:** Shorthand, internal jargon, and unexplained references are not acceptable in governed material, even if they're how the team talks day to day.
